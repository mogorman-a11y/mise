Private Chef HACCP Checklist UK

If you work as a private chef, your food-safety system needs to show not only that you understand the risks, but that your controls are actually being followed. This checklist covers the core control areas and the records a UK private chef should consider as part of a HACCP-based food-safety system — with legal requirements, FSA and Food Standards Scotland guidance, and sensible operational choices kept clearly apart.

Short answer: there is no single official form or fixed list of daily logs that every private chef must complete by law. What UK law requires is that you have food-safety management procedures based on HACCP principles, that you monitor your controls, and that you keep records where your procedures call for one and where a record is the practical way to show the control was applied. The checklist below is a working starting point, not a legal minimum.

How to read the labels in this article

  • Legal A requirement that arises from UK food law. Specific exemptions, tolerances and statutory defences can apply, so treat these as the direction of travel, not a trap.
  • Guidance Official Food Standards Agency or Food Standards Scotland advice — the recommended way to meet the law, not the only lawful method.
  • Operational A sensible working choice you make and write into your own procedure.
  • Veriqo default A threshold the Veriqo app ships with and you can change in Settings — a product setting, not the law.

Private chef HACCP checklist — at a glance

Work through these control areas when you build or review your food-safety system. Most private chefs will need something in every area, but the depth of record for each depends on your menus, how you work, and what your written procedure says.

  • Food-business registration and business details
  • Written HACCP-based food-safety procedures
  • Approved / reputable suppliers
  • Delivery checks — condition, packaging, dates, temperature
  • Chilled and frozen storage control
  • Cooking and reheating control
  • Cooling of food cooked in advance
  • Allergen information and cross-contact control
  • Cleaning and disinfection
  • Separation of raw and ready-to-eat food
  • Personal hygiene and fitness to work
  • Pest awareness at your base and on site
  • Transport of chilled, frozen and hot food
  • Client-kitchen / venue assessment
  • Corrective actions when a check fails
  • Traceability / supplier information
  • Review and update of your procedures

The boxes above are a visual checklist for planning, not an interactive form and not a legal form. Nothing in UK food law says each of these items needs an individual written log every day. What matters is that the control exists, you check it, and you can show it.

HACCP in practical terms

HACCP sounds industrial, but for a private chef it comes down to a short loop you run for each real hazard in your work:

  1. Hazard — what could make someone ill (for example, bacterial growth in chilled ingredients).
  2. Control — what you do about it (keep chilled food under appropriate temperature control).
  3. Monitoring — how you check the control is working (check storage and transport temperature as appropriate to the food and the journey).
  4. Corrective action — what you do if it fails (assess the food, move it to working refrigeration, or discard it — depending on how far and how long it was out of temperature, following your procedure).
  5. Evidence & review — record the check and any corrective action where your system requires it, and revisit the procedure when your menus, process or working conditions change.

GOV.UK sets out the same idea as seven things a food-safety management system does: identify food hazards, identify the critical control points, set limits for them, monitor them, put checks in place that the system is working, put things right when there is a problem, and “keep records of your food safety systems, checks and how you’ve put things right”.

Control, monitoring and evidence are three different things

This distinction is the part most private chefs get wrong, in both directions — either recording nothing, or trying to log every action every time.

  • Having a control means there is a defined way you manage the hazard. Every significant hazard needs one. Controls are not optional.
  • Monitoring the control means you actually check it is working — a probe reading, a look at the fridge display, a check that the delivery is cold and in date.
  • Recording evidence means writing down the result where your procedure calls for it, or where a record is the sensible way to prove the control was applied. You do not need a timestamped entry for every wipe of a surface or every plate that leaves the pass.

An Environmental Health Officer is looking for a system that is real and used. A failed check with no note of what you did about it is weak evidence. A tidy pile of “all OK” logs with no corrective actions and no reviews can look like a form-filling exercise. Aim for records that a stranger could read and understand what happened.

Record areas, one by one

1. Suppliers and deliveries

Buy from reputable, traceable suppliers. When food that matters to safety is delivered, check the condition, the packaging, the use-by and best-before dates, and — for chilled and frozen items — the temperature, to the extent that is practical for how you buy. If a delivery is not acceptable, reject it (or use only the parts that are safe) and note what you did.

Under assimilated Regulation (EC) 178/2002, you must be able to identify your immediate suppliers — “one step back”. In practice, keeping supplier invoices and delivery notes usually satisfies this; they show the supplier, what was supplied, and when. Food you serve directly to a client’s guests goes to the final consumer, so there is no “one step forward” customer record to keep.

2. Fridge and freezer control

Chilled and frozen food must be kept under temperature control. The practical checks are your own fridge and freezer before a job, and the client’s fridge when you arrive and again when you load it.

Guidance 5°C or below is the FSA recommended operating target for chilled food. Legal 8°C or below is the cold-holding maximum in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, subject to the applicable rules and time-limited exceptions; Scotland has equivalent temperature-control requirements. Freezer storage is usually run at around −18°C. Rather than repeat the figures here, use the UK Food Temperature Guide, which sets out every temperature with its legal or guidance status, and the printable HACCP temperature log if you want a paper sheet.

3. Cooking and reheating

Cook and reheat food thoroughly enough to control harmful bacteria. This is time and temperature, not a single magic number: UK FSA guidance recognises a set of equivalent combinations — for example 70°C for 2 minutes, 75°C for 30 seconds, or 80°C for 6 seconds — and 75°C at the centre as a common instantaneous check. It is not correct to say 75°C is “the legal cooking temperature”. In Scotland, reheating has its own rule (82°C, with a quality defence).

Veriqo default The Veriqo app uses 75°C as its default cooking and reheating check. That is a product default you can change, not a legal threshold. The full picture — including the Scottish position — is in the UK Food Temperature Guide. Record probe checks for the higher-risk items: poultry, mince, sausages, burgers, rolled or reformed joints, and anything reheated.

4. Cooling

If you cook food in advance, cool it as quickly as possible and refrigerate it promptly. Current FSA / GOV.UK guidance describes getting cooked food into the fridge within one to two hours where appropriate. There is no single fixed cooling time set in the general food-hygiene regulations, so do not treat one as law. Help it along with smaller portions, shallow trays, stirring, and an ice-water bath or blast chiller if you have one. A short cooling note — what, when it started, when it went under refrigeration — is worth keeping for food you cool to use later.

5. Allergens

You must be able to give accurate allergen information for every dish, and you must control cross-contact. That means knowing the allergen content of your recipes, updating it when ingredients or recipes change, checking supplier information, asking each client about requirements before the booking, and providing the information in writing where that is appropriate. Keep a record of what you told the client and any steps you took to avoid cross-contact.

Allergens have their own detail — the 14 regulated allergens, what “providing information” means in practice, Natasha’s Law and the records that protect you. See the private chef’s guide to allergen management rather than duplicating it here.

6. Cleaning and disinfection

Food-contact surfaces and equipment need to be cleaned and then disinfected; other areas need to be kept clean. A written cleaning schedule — what is cleaned, how, how often, and with what product — is the practical way to show this is being done, without implying that every cleaning action needs a timestamped record. Cover work surfaces, boards, knives and small equipment, cloths (and how you keep clean and dirty ones apart), fridges, and your transport boxes. Follow the contact time on the disinfectant you use.

7. Personal hygiene and fitness to work

Wash hands thoroughly and often, especially after handling raw food, waste or your phone. Wear clean protective clothing where appropriate. Cover cuts and wounds with a coloured waterproof dressing. Do not handle open food when you or anyone helping you has symptoms of a food-borne illness: FSA guidance is that anyone with diarrhoea and/or vomiting should stay away from open food, normally until 48 hours after symptoms stop naturally. Keep a brief note if you exclude someone from a job for this reason.

8. Pest issues

Most private chefs do not run a permanent restaurant kitchen, so a contracted pest-control company is not automatically required. What is required is that food is protected from pests wherever you store and prepare it. In practice: keep dry goods sealed and off the floor at your base, look for droppings, gnaw marks, insects or nests, keep waste managed, and be alert to signs in client kitchens and temporary venues. If you see evidence of pests, protect or discard the affected food, deal with the source, and note what you did.

9. Transport

Transport is where private-chef food safety is most often lost, and it is a control area a fixed restaurant does not have. Keep chilled food cold and frozen food frozen in transit, using pre-chilled insulated boxes and ice packs, and keep journeys as short as you can. Keep raw and ready-to-eat food separated and protected from contamination. For hot food carried hot, the relevant control is the hot-holding standard (above 63°C) — but transport itself is covered by the general duty to keep food safe, not a separate universal transport-thermometer law, and for longer journeys it is often safer to carry food chilled and reheat it fully on site.

Check and record temperature for journeys where temperature control matters — typically a check as you load and another on arrival — and note any corrective action. Again, the figures live in the UK Food Temperature Guide.

10. Client kitchen / venue check

This is the check most generic HACCP systems miss, and the one that most often saves a private chef. Before you start work in an unfamiliar kitchen, run a quick assessment and note the outcome:

  • Is there enough working fridge space, and is it holding temperature?
  • Freezer capacity, if the menu needs it?
  • Handwashing facilities with hot water and soap, separate from where you prep?
  • A supply of drinkable (potable) water?
  • A clean, clear preparation surface?
  • Oven and hob that actually work and reach temperature?
  • Somewhere to use and clean a probe thermometer?
  • Cross-contamination risks — the client’s own open food, raw meat in the same fridge, shared boards?
  • Pets in the kitchen, or signs of pests?
  • Waste arrangements, and enough power for your equipment?

If the kitchen cannot support the menu safely, change the plan — more prep at your base, a simpler dish, or a serving approach that does not depend on the failing equipment. Frame this as practical risk management for the job, not a statutory form.

11. Corrective actions

Recording a failed check without recording what you did about it is one of the weakest positions to be in. For each failure, note what failed, what you did, and the outcome — ideally attached to the check itself. Realistic examples:

  • A delivery arrived warm, so it was rejected and the supplier informed.
  • A client fridge was running warm, so food was moved to a working fridge and its time out of temperature assessed before use.
  • Cooked food did not reach the target on the probe, so it went back on the heat and was re-checked.
  • Food that had been in the danger zone too long was assessed and discarded.
  • An oven fault was escalated to the client and the menu adjusted.
  • Allergen uncertainty about a bought-in component was resolved with the supplier before service.

Do not write down a corrective action you would not actually take, and never record an unsafe one to make the paperwork tidy.

12. Review

Your procedures are not a one-off document. GOV.UK guidance is to “check, review and update your system regularly, and whenever you change your food processes or suppliers”. For a private chef, that means reviewing when you:

  • change your menu or a cooking process;
  • buy new equipment, or start using a new transport method;
  • move to a new base or prep kitchen;
  • have an incident, a complaint, or a check that keeps failing;
  • see a change in official guidance or the law.

A dated note of what you changed and why is enough.

How long should I keep HACCP records?

There is no single universal retention period in the general food-hygiene regulations that applies to every private-chef HACCP record. Keep records long enough to demonstrate that your controls are being followed, and to satisfy any specific legal, customer, contractual or business requirement that applies to you — and keep them available for inspection. Traceability records (supplier information) should be kept at least until it can reasonably be assumed the food has been consumed. Allergen information should be accurate and kept up to date.

Do not treat “three months” as a blanket rule, and there is no general rule that allergen records must always be retained longer than everything else — keep what you need to protect yourself, and keep the underlying allergen information current. If a client or agency contract sets a retention period, follow that.

Paper or digital?

The law asks for a food-safety system that is appropriate to your business, actually followed, and capable of being shown to an inspector — not a particular format. Paper works. Digital HACCP records are not legally required.

Where digital helps is the practical side: timestamps that are hard to argue with, fast retrieval when an EHO asks, consistency between jobs, corrective actions captured against the check they belong to, records tied to a specific booking, and less scrambling before an inspection. If you cook in several kitchens a week and lose the odd paper sheet, that is the case for going digital.

Knowing what to record is one thing. Keeping it organised across every booking is another.

Frequently asked questions

Do private chefs need HACCP in the UK?

Yes. Any food business operator must have food-safety management procedures based on HACCP principles, and a private chef cooking for paying clients is a food business — whether you work from your own kitchen or a client’s home. Our guide on whether private chefs need HACCP covers the legal basis in detail.

What HACCP records should a private chef keep?

Records that show your controls are being followed: deliveries and supplier information, chilled and frozen storage checks, cooking and reheating checks for higher-risk food, cooling where you cook ahead, allergen information, a cleaning schedule, staff-illness exclusions, pest sightings, transport checks where temperature matters, a quick kitchen assessment for unfamiliar venues, and the corrective action taken whenever a check fails. There is no fixed legal list — your written procedure defines what you record and how often. The records summary table is a planning starting point.

Do I need to record fridge temperatures every day?

There is no universal legal rule that a private chef must record a fridge temperature every calendar day. Your procedure sets the frequency. A common, defensible approach is to check and record the fridge at the start of each job and when you load a client’s fridge, with a corrective-action note if it is out of range. 5°C or below is the FSA recommended target; 8°C is the legal cold-holding maximum in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, subject to the applicable rules and exceptions.

Do I need to record every cooking temperature?

No. There is no requirement to probe and log every dish at every service. Focus on higher-risk food — poultry, mince, rolled or reformed joints, reheated food — and record enough probe checks to show the control is applied consistently. UK guidance recognises equivalent time/temperature combinations rather than one lawful number; see the UK Food Temperature Guide.

Do private chefs need a cleaning schedule?

A cleaning schedule — what is cleaned, how, how often, with what — is the practical way to show cleaning and disinfection are happening. It does not mean every wipe of a surface needs a timestamped entry. Disinfection of food-contact surfaces and equipment is the priority.

How long should I keep HACCP records?

There is no single universal retention period in the general food-hygiene regulations. Keep records long enough to demonstrate your controls are followed and to meet any specific legal, customer, contractual or business requirement — and keep them available for inspection. Keep traceability (supplier) records at least until the food can reasonably be assumed to have been consumed. Keep allergen information accurate and current.

Can HACCP records be digital?

Yes. Environmental Health Officers accept digital records as readily as paper, provided they are complete, dated and available on request. Digital HACCP is not legally required — it just makes timestamps, retrieval, consistency, corrective actions and linking records to a booking easier.

What will an EHO want to see?

Evidence that a food-safety management system exists and is genuinely used — your HACCP-based procedures, the monitoring records they call for, and the corrective actions taken when something went wrong. See what an EHO inspector actually checks.

Do I need HACCP records when cooking in a client’s home?

Yes. Your obligations as a food business operator do not change with location. The practical control is a quick assessment of the kitchen you are about to use, recorded briefly for each unfamiliar venue.
