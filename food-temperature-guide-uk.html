UK Food Temperature Guide

A practical reference for private chefs and small food businesses — with UK food-safety guidance, legal limits and sensible operating targets clearly separated.

This is a practical summary for training and day-to-day use — not legal advice. Food law and official guidance change, and some rules differ between England, Wales, Northern Ireland and Scotland. Check the primary sources at the foot of this page for the current position that applies to you. Veriqo is not a government scheme, and using it does not by itself make a business compliant. England, Wales and Northern Ireland vs Scotland

Most temperatures on this page apply across the UK, but there are differences. The clearest is reheating: in England, Wales and Northern Ireland there is no fixed reheating temperature in law, and FSA guidance is 75°C or piping hot throughout; in Scotland, regulations require 82°C unless that would spoil the food. The time-limited exceptions to hot and cold holding described above are the England, Wales and Northern Ireland rules — check Food Standards Scotland for the equivalent Scottish detail. Always confirm which nation's rules apply to where you are working.

The time-limited exceptions, in plain terms

Hot food that is being kept hot must normally be at 63°C or above. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland it may be below 63°C for a single continuous period of up to two hours — for example while it is on a buffet — after which it must be used, cooled and refrigerated, or thrown away. Chilled food that needs temperature control must normally be at 8°C or below, but may be above 8°C for a single continuous period of up to four hours for service or display, after which it must be returned to 8°C or below or discarded. These are single periods, not something to repeat. Further statutory defences (for example a manufacturer-specified temperature, or an unavoidable reason such as equipment breakdown) can also apply — see the regulations.

Why 70°C for 2 minutes and 75°C for 30 seconds count as equivalent

The destruction of harmful bacteria depends on both temperature and time. A lower temperature held for longer can achieve the same result as a higher temperature reached briefly. FSA guidance therefore lists a set of combinations — including 60°C for 45 minutes, 65°C for 10 minutes, 70°C for 2 minutes, 75°C for 30 seconds and 80°C for 6 seconds — as broadly equivalent ways to cook food safely. A single reading of 75°C at the centre is the version most caterers use because it is quick to check.

Understanding the numbers

Why one temperature is not always the whole answer

Killing harmful bacteria depends on both how hot food gets and how long it stays there. A lower temperature held for longer can be as effective as a higher temperature reached for an instant. That is why FSA guidance lists several time/temperature combinations — 70°C for 2 minutes, 75°C for 30 seconds, 80°C for 6 seconds, 60°C for 45 minutes — as broadly equivalent. A single probe reading of 75°C at the centre is a convenient shorthand most caterers use because it is easy to check in a busy kitchen, but it is not the only lawful way to cook food safely.

What temperature should a private chef use as an operational limit?

Pick one clear standard and apply it every time. For most private chefs that is “75°C at the centre, or piping hot throughout” for cooking and reheating, “5°C or below” for the fridge, and “63°C or above” for anything held hot. Writing your chosen limits into your food safety management system and checking against them consistently is what an Environmental Health Officer wants to see — not a different number every service. Keep the underlying guidance in mind so you can explain why your limit is safe, and tighten it for higher-risk dishes.

What happens if a temperature check fails?

There is no single fix — it depends on the food and where it went wrong. Some practical examples: if cooked food is below your limit, keep cooking and re-check; if a fridge is running warm, move stock to another unit, judge whether the food has been too warm for too long, and get the fridge looked at; if hot food has dropped below 63°C, reheat it thoroughly (to 75°C, or 82°C in Scotland) if it is still within the permitted time, otherwise discard it; if chilled food has been in the danger zone too long, throw it away rather than risk it. Whatever you decide, write down what happened and what you did.

What should I record for HACCP?

For each check: what was checked (the dish, the fridge, the delivery), the result (the reading), the date and time, any corrective action taken, and — where more than one person works the job — who did the check. Records do not need to be long, but they must be complete, honest and up to date. Keep them long enough to show your food-safety controls are being followed, and to meet any specific legal, customer or business requirements that apply to you; there is no single retention period fixed in the general food hygiene regulations. Allergen information must be accurate, provided in writing where appropriate, and kept up to date. Digital records are fine; an Environmental Health Officer will accept them as readily as paper.

02

Food temperatures in a client's kitchen

A private chef carries risks a fixed restaurant does not. You are often using a fridge you have never seen, with an unknown service history and a thermostat that may be optimistic. You have transported food in a car boot. Service can be delayed while guests finish their drinks. And you cannot always drop something into a blast chiller.

Practical steps that make the difference:

  • Check the client fridge's temperature when you arrive, and again before you load it. If it will not hold temperature, change the plan.
  • Bring your own probe thermometer, cool boxes and ice packs — do not rely on the kitchen having them.
  • Pre-chill or pre-heat your transport containers so food is not warming or cooling on the journey.
  • Note when food left your kitchen and when it went back under temperature control, so you can account for the danger-zone time.
  • Build in a margin: treat “5°C” as the target, not “just under 8°C”, because a strange fridge and a warm room eat the margin quickly.
  • If guests are running late, keep hot food at 63°C or above and cold food at 8°C or below, and watch the two-hour and four-hour limits.

Using a probe thermometer

Probe the thickest part or the centre of the food — the slowest point to heat through, and for chilled checks the point most likely to be warm. Do not let the tip touch bone, the tray or the pan, or you will read the metal, not the food. For a whole bird, probe the thickest part of the thigh and the breast, away from the cavity. Clean and disinfect the probe before and after use, and always between raw and cooked food, using probe wipes or 70% alcohol wipes. Check the probe's accuracy from time to time and after any knock: a slush of ice and water should read about 0°C, allowing roughly ±1°C. Everyday catering does not need laboratory calibration, but a probe that has drifted is worse than no probe.

Food temperature FAQs

What temperature should cooked food reach in the UK?

There is no single cooking temperature set in law for England, Wales and Northern Ireland. FSA guidance recognises several equivalent time/temperature combinations, such as 70°C for 2 minutes, 75°C for 30 seconds or 80°C for 6 seconds. Many caterers use one instant reading of 75°C at the centre, or “piping hot throughout”, as a simple check.

Is 75°C a legal requirement?

No. 75°C is a common operational check and a convenient shorthand, not a figure fixed in law for England, Wales and Northern Ireland. The legal duty is to cook food so that harmful bacteria are destroyed, and FSA guidance describes time/temperature combinations that achieve this. Veriqo uses 75°C as an adjustable default check, not a legal threshold.

What temperature should a commercial fridge be?

The FSA recommends chilled food is kept at 5°C or below. The legal maximum for cold-holding food that needs temperature control is 8°C or below in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, with a similar requirement in Scotland. Aiming for 5°C leaves a safety margin.

Is 5°C the legal fridge limit in the UK?

No. 5°C is the FSA's recommended target. The legal cold-holding maximum is 8°C in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Food may be above 8°C for a single period of up to 4 hours on display, after which it must be chilled or discarded.

What temperature should hot food be held at?

63°C or above. This is a legal minimum for hot holding before service. In England, Wales and Northern Ireland food may be kept below 63°C for a single period of up to two hours, after which it must be used, chilled, or thrown away.

What temperature should reheated food reach?

In England, Wales and Northern Ireland there is no fixed reheating temperature in law; FSA guidance is to reheat thoroughly to 75°C or until piping hot throughout, and to reheat food once only. In Scotland, regulations require reheating to 82°C unless that would unacceptably spoil the food.

What temperature should frozen food be stored at?

−18°C or below. This is the recommended standard, and commercially quick-frozen foods are legally required to be held at −18°C, with small permitted tolerances during transport and display.

How should a private chef record temperatures?

Record what was checked, the reading, the date and time, any corrective action, and who did the check if more than one person is working. Keep records long enough to show your controls are being followed and to meet any legal, customer or business requirements that apply to you — there is no single retention period set in the general food hygiene regulations. Allergen information must be accurate, written where appropriate, and kept up to date. See our printable temperature log.

Can HACCP temperature records be digital?

Yes. Environmental Health Officers accept digital records as readily as paper, provided they are complete, time-stamped and available on request. Veriqo HACCP produces EHO-ready PDF and CSV exports.
